The late afternoon sun bleeds orange and rose across the dusty courtyard of a crumbling haveli. A woman, her face partially veiled, sits cross-legged on a worn, intricately patterned rug. Around her, scattered across the stone floor, are piles of dried rose petals, vibrant crimson against the grey. She’s meticulously sorting them, separating the perfect blooms from the bruised and faded ones, her fingers moving with a practiced, almost ritualistic grace. A young boy, no older than seven, watches her intently, occasionally picking up a petal and offering it to her with a shy smile. The air is thick with the scent of dried roses and a quiet, melancholic stillness, punctuated only by the distant call to prayer and the rustling of the wind through the overgrown bougainvillea climbing the haveli walls.
